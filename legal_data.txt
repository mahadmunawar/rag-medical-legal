A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties that is enforceable by law. For a contract to be valid, it must contain mutual assent, often described as a "meeting of the minds." This begins with an Offer by one party and an unconditional Acceptance of those specific terms by the other. Furthermore, the contract requires Consideration, meaning a bargained-for exchange of legal value, such as money, goods, services, or a promise not to do something one has a legal right to do. 

Beyond offer, acceptance, and consideration, a valid contract requires that all parties possess legal capacity. This means the individuals must be of sound mind, not acting under duress, and generally of legal age (18 years or older). A contract involving minors or mentally incapacitated individuals may be deemed voidable. Additionally, the contract must have a lawful objective; any agreement formed for the purpose of committing an illegal act, or an act contrary to public policy, is fundamentally void and unenforceable in any jurisdiction.

When a party fails to fulfill their obligations under a contract, it is legally termed a Breach of Contract. A "material breach" is a severe violation that strikes at the heart of the agreement, excusing the non-breaching party from their obligations and allowing them to seek damages. A "minor breach" means the core of the contract was fulfilled, but a slight deviation occurred. Remedies for a breach include compensatory damages (monetary compensation for actual losses), liquidated damages (pre-agreed penalty sums), or specific performance, where the court orders the breaching party to fulfill their exact contractual duties.

Civil litigation is the legal process through which parties seek monetary damages or specific performance to resolve disputes, entirely separate from criminal sanctions. The lifecycle of a civil lawsuit begins with the plaintiff filing a Summons and Complaint. The defendant must then file an Answer. The most time-consuming phase is Discovery, where both sides are legally compelled to exchange evidence through depositions (oral testimony), interrogatories (written questions), and requests for document production. If the dispute is not resolved via mediation or summary judgment, it proceeds to a formal trial.

Tort law addresses civil wrongs that cause a claimant to suffer loss or harm, resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. The most common cause of action in tort law is Negligence. To successfully litigate a negligence claim, the plaintiff must definitively prove four elements: Duty (the defendant owed a standard of care), Breach (the defendant failed to meet that standard), Causation (the breach was the direct and proximate cause of the injury), and Damages (the plaintiff suffered actual, measurable harm or financial loss).
