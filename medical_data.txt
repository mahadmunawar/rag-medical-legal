Cancer is a complex group of diseases characterized by unrelenting cellular proliferation, avoidance of growth suppression signals, and resistance to apoptosis (programmed cell death). In a healthy body, cell division is strictly regulated by a balance of oncogenes, which promote cell growth, and tumor suppressor genes, such as p53, which inhibit growth and trigger cell death if DNA damage is detected. When these genes undergo mutations—due to inherited genetics, environmental carcinogens, radiation, or viruses—cells begin to divide uncontrollably, leading to the formation of a malignant neoplasm or tumor.

The tumor microenvironment plays a critical role in cancer pathophysiology. It consists of non-malignant stromal cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells. Instead of fighting the cancer, this microenvironment is often hijacked by the tumor to support its own growth. Malignant tumors also secrete factors that stimulate angiogenesis, the physiological process through which new blood vessels form from pre-existing vessels. This neovascularization provides the growing tumor with the dedicated oxygen and nutrients it needs to survive and expand.

The severity and progression of cancer are universally classified using the TNM staging system. "T" describes the size of the primary tumor and whether it has invaded nearby tissue. "N" indicates whether regional lymph nodes are involved. "M" signifies metastasis, the process by which cancer cells break away from the primary tumor, travel through the blood or lymphatic system, and form new tumors in distant organs like the liver, lungs, or bones. Stage I and II cancers are localized, whereas Stage IV universally indicates distant metastatic spread.

Modern oncology utilizes several systemic and localized modalities for treatment. Surgical resection aims to physically remove the primary tumor mass. Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing beams to induce lethal DNA damage in localized cancer cells. Chemotherapy involves systemic cytotoxic drugs that target rapidly dividing cells throughout the body. More recently, targeted therapy focuses on specific molecular pathways essential for cancer survival, while immunotherapy utilizes checkpoint inhibitors to unmask cancer cells, training the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy the malignancy.
